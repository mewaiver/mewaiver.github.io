The checkout API is slower, but its error rate has barely moved. A gateway dashboard shows a rise in tail latency; the authentication and order services are healthy by their own availability measures. An engineer opens a slow trace and finds a long call to a tax provider. Is the provider slow, or did the order service wait for a connection before sending anything? The distinction determines which team can fix the incident.

This is a **hypothetical** incident, and the figures below are illustrative. Its ambiguity is real: a distributed request crosses processes, queues, network links, and systems with different clocks and owners. Telemetry records selected observations at those boundaries. It does not automatically turn them into a causal explanation.

## A symptom is not a diagnosis

Monitoring answers a bounded operational question: did a known measure cross a threshold, or did users experience a service-level failure? A latency alert can be valuable precisely because it does not presume a cause. Google's [SRE guidance on monitoring distributed systems](https://sre.google/sre-book/monitoring-distributed-systems/) distinguishes user-visible symptoms from possible causes and recommends paging on actionable symptoms. Observability is the broader ability to investigate behavior, including failure modes the team did not anticipate when it built its dashboards.

That distinction matters when service-level charts disagree. The gateway can report slow checkouts while the order service reports low CPU and few errors. Neither chart is wrong. CPU says little about time spent waiting for a client connection, an upstream response, or a lock. Averages can also conceal a small, badly affected cohort. Even a gateway's 95th percentile and an order service's 95th percentile cannot be added or treated as the same request: each summarizes a different population. Diagnosis requires narrowing the affected traffic by route, time, deployment, region, or dependency, then following actual requests.

Metrics, logs, and traces make different parts of that work possible. Metrics aggregate counts and distributions cheaply enough to show when a regression began, how widespread it is, and whether a mitigation changed it. They usually discard the identity and sequence of individual requests. Logs preserve selected events and application decisions, such as a retry, rejected connection, or database timeout, but an uncorrelated log line can be difficult to place in a particular request. Traces represent the path of an operation through instrumented components, with timing and relationships; they cannot describe work that was never instrumented, and a long span does not by itself identify the source of the delay. The three signals are complementary evidence, not three independent proofs of root cause.

## Follow one request, then test whether it is typical

Suppose the gateway's checkout 95th-percentile latency rises from an illustrative 200 milliseconds to 900 milliseconds, while its request rate and error rate remain close to baseline. The engineer first compares checkout with other routes and checks whether the change coincides with a deployment, a traffic shift, or a dependency alarm. A histogram for outbound tax calls shows its slow tail growing during the same interval. That is a useful lead, but correlation in time cannot establish whether the provider, the client, or increased load upstream caused the slowdown.

One sampled slow request takes 1.1 seconds at the gateway. Its child spans show authentication taking 25 milliseconds, the order service doing a 30-millisecond database call, and an outbound tax-client operation occupying 800 milliseconds. The trace directs attention toward the tax boundary. It does not mean the provider spent 800 milliseconds computing tax. Depending on where the client library starts and ends its span, that interval could include connection-pool wait, DNS or connection setup, network delay, provider processing, response transfer, or retries. Parent spans also include child time, and concurrent child spans can overlap; summing every displayed duration would double-count work.

The next step is to compare many slow and normal traces from the same route and interval, checking whether the tax-client span is consistently long and whether retry counts, client connection-pool occupancy, and queue-wait measurements rise together. Correlated order-service logs might show repeated attempts or a pool-acquisition timeout; provider-side timestamps, if available, might show how long the provider actually handled requests. If provider processing stays near baseline while local pool wait rises, pool saturation becomes a stronger hypothesis. To confirm it, the team would examine pool limits, active and pending connections, recent client configuration changes, and the effect of a controlled mitigation. If provider-side processing also rises, the same trace supports a different investigation. A database span of 30 milliseconds in one request does not clear the database for the entire incident; aggregate database latency and pool-wait data still matter.

This sequence also exposes a common source of false certainty. A trace is one observed execution, often selected by a sampling policy. An alert is an aggregate over many executions. They refer to different populations until the engineer checks that the trace's route, time, region, version, and outcome match the affected cohort. A set of traces retained because they were slow cannot establish how frequently slowness occurs; a request metric covering the full traffic is needed for that. A convincing explanation accounts for the broad regression and survives a test against an alternative cause.

## The trace depends on context crossing every boundary

A trace is a collection of spans, each representing an operation with its own timing, attributes, span ID, and usually a parent span ID. A shared trace ID groups spans; parent-child relationships show which recorded operation invoked another. OpenTelemetry's [trace data model](https://opentelemetry.io/docs/specs/otel/trace/data-model/) also allows span links for relationships that do not fit a simple parent-child chain, as can happen with queued or batched work. These relationships describe recorded causality, not every dependency in the system.

To carry the relationship from gateway to authentication or order service, instrumentation injects trace context into the outbound request and the receiving service extracts it before creating a child span. The [W3C Trace Context specification](https://www.w3.org/TR/trace-context/) defines the HTTP `traceparent` header; OpenTelemetry's [context-propagation guide](https://opentelemetry.io/docs/concepts/context-propagation/) explains how the IDs are carried across service boundaries. A proxy that drops that header, a custom message consumer that fails to extract it, or asynchronous work that loses its in-process context can start a new root trace. The resulting fragments may each look internally correct while hiding the connection the investigator needs.

Missing spans are a different failure. A service can propagate context but fail to export its span, leaving later spans with the same trace ID and a parent that cannot be found. Conversely, an uninstrumented database pool may leave a large unexplained interval inside an order-service span. The OpenTelemetry data model explicitly distinguishes [context propagation from span export](https://opentelemetry.io/docs/specs/otel/trace/data-model/). Engineers should check both before reading a blank stretch of a trace as idle time.

Logs need the same discipline. Recording trace and span IDs in structured logs lets an investigator move from a tax-client span to its retry or pool-wait events; OpenTelemetry's [log data model](https://opentelemetry.io/docs/specs/otel/logs/data-model/) includes optional fields for those IDs. Correlation fails if a log line is emitted outside the active context, the service uses inconsistent field names, or the relevant log was discarded. A trace ID is an index into evidence, not evidence that a particular event occurred.

## The collection pipeline has its own failure modes

OpenTelemetry supplies APIs, SDKs, instrumentation, semantic conventions, propagation, and a collection protocol. Its [Collector](https://opentelemetry.io/docs/collector/) can receive, process, and export telemetry through configured pipelines. A storage and query backend, plus any visualization platform, is still needed to search traces and logs or display dashboards. Adopting OpenTelemetry therefore does not automatically provide a working investigation workflow.

Automatic instrumentation can expose HTTP and database calls quickly, but it may miss proprietary clients, in-process queues, or business decisions. Manual spans can fill those gaps, provided teams choose meaningful operation boundaries and keep attributes consistent across services. Before rollout, engineers should test propagation through gateways, proxies, message brokers, and background jobs; check that logs carry the intended context; and compare trace timing with independent client and server measurements. Collector queues, exporter failures, and dropped spans also need monitoring. The [Collector scaling guide](https://opentelemetry.io/docs/collector/scaling/) describes queue growth and refused telemetry as observable pipeline conditions. A telemetry outage during a service incident is itself a diagnostic risk.

Collection must also have a budget. In a Prometheus-style metric, each distinct label-value combination creates another time series. A request ID or user ID as a metric label can make series counts grow with traffic; route templates and bounded status categories are more useful for aggregate diagnosis. [Prometheus's metric-naming guidance](https://prometheus.io/docs/practices/naming/) explicitly warns against unbounded labels. High-cardinality identifiers are often better kept in appropriately protected logs or trace attributes, where they can help find a request without multiplying metric series.

Trace sampling trades storage and ingestion cost against the chance of retaining a rare failure. An early, or head, decision is comparatively cheap but cannot know that a request will later be slow or fail. A tail decision can retain traces based on their eventual latency or errors, but it needs state and enough time to receive relevant spans; late arrivals and overloaded collectors can still leave incomplete evidence. OpenTelemetry's [sampling guidance](https://opentelemetry.io/docs/concepts/sampling/) describes both approaches and their operational costs. Teams should choose a policy for the failures they actually need to investigate and verify, with deliberate fault tests, that those traces survive the pipeline.

More data can make diagnosis worse as well as more expensive. Verbose logs create search noise; broad alerts train responders to ignore pages; long retention increases storage cost and the consequences of exposing credentials or personal data. OpenTelemetry's [sensitive-data guidance](https://opentelemetry.io/docs/security/handling-sensitive-data/) notes that instrumentation can collect information the operator did not intend and that the operator must decide what is sensitive. Attribute allowlists, redaction close to collection, access controls, and retention periods should follow the diagnostic purpose of each signal. Instrumentation and collection also consume CPU, memory, and network capacity, so teams should measure their overhead under load rather than assume it is free.

## Build evidence for the decisions incidents require

The engineering objective is not to maximize telemetry volume. It is to preserve enough trustworthy evidence to decide whether to roll back an order-service release, enlarge a saturated client pool, reduce concurrency, or escalate to the tax provider. That suggests a deliberate order of investment: alert on user-visible symptoms; maintain bounded metrics for the affected cohorts; instrument the boundaries where ownership or latency can change; preserve trace and log correlation; then test what an investigator can actually infer when a dependency slows or a context header disappears. The right sampling rate, retention period, and span detail depend on traffic, incident frequency, privacy obligations, and the cost of being unable to explain a failure.

Distributed systems remain hard to diagnose because their behavior is jointly produced across boundaries while each signal records only a partial view. A useful observability system does more than collect those views: it preserves their relationships, exposes where evidence is missing, and lets engineers distinguish a slow dependency from a slow path to that dependency. The decisive improvement is the ability to test a causal claim against independent measurements, including the possibility that the most attractive trace tells the wrong story.
