In 2024, software company 37signals said its annual cloud bill had fallen from a previous $3.2 million run rate to $1.3 million after moving seven applications, including HEY, from AWS to its own servers. It reported spending about $700,000 on new hardware. The qualification matters as much as the headline: the company already had data center racks, power capacity, and an operations team. The figures are [37signals' own account](https://world.hey.com/dhh/our-cloud-exit-savings-will-now-top-ten-million-over-five-years-c7d9b5bd), and the difference between two cloud bills is not, by itself, an independently audited comparison of total operating costs.

The case points to a workload-level question: **under what conditions does a particular workload run better, or cost less over its useful life, on infrastructure its operator controls?** Cloud repatriation means moving an existing public cloud workload or its data to owned, leased, or privately operated infrastructure. It can be selective; an organization may move a database while leaving other services in a public cloud. That choice creates a new architecture to operate, not merely a change of address.

## The economic case depends on utilization

Public cloud turns much infrastructure spending into a variable bill. That is valuable when demand is uncertain, capacity must appear quickly, or usage rises and falls sharply. A data center commits the operator to capacity before demand arrives. If a service uses that capacity consistently, the fixed investment can be spread across a large amount of work; if utilization is low, idle servers still consume money and attention.

Start with the workload's actual monthly usage, not a provider's list price or a server quote. Include compute, databases, storage, backups, support, licenses, and network traffic in the cloud baseline. Separate recurring application traffic from one-time migration traffic. Then price a comparable local service: hardware or lease payments, maintenance, rack space, power, cooling, network links, software licenses, replacement cycles, staffing, spare capacity, and a second site or other disaster recovery arrangement where the service requires one. Migration engineering and a period of running both environments also belong in the comparison. The useful denominator is often cost per transaction, user, or unit of stored data at the required service level, not the monthly invoice alone.

The cloud baseline deserves its own optimization pass. Underused instances, unsuitable storage tiers, and demand that can be covered by commitment discounts can change the result without changing location. In the [FinOps Foundation's 2025 practitioner survey](https://data.finops.org/2025-report/), workload optimization and waste reduction were respondents' highest priority. That finding describes the survey participants' priorities; it does not establish that repatriation saves money for a typical organization.

37signals illustrates both the opportunity and the limit of the comparison. Its reported reduction in cloud spending followed a move to hardware that fit within existing rack and power limits, and it said it did not enlarge its operations team. A company that must build those capabilities from scratch faces a different cost curve. One-time migration traffic can also have different pricing from ordinary egress: [AWS announced a waiver for eligible migration transfers in March 2024](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/aws/free-data-transfer-out-to-internet-when-moving-out-of-aws/). The terms of any waiver need checking for the specific move. Ongoing traffic between a retained cloud service and a repatriated system is a separate, potentially recurring cost.

## The workload boundary can matter more than the server

Latency depends on where users, data, and dependent systems sit, and on the number and duration of calls between them. Moving an application close to a factory controller or a large local data set may remove a costly network hop. Moving its database away from application servers still in the cloud may add one on every request. A nearby cloud region might serve end users better than a distant corporate data center.

This is why the unit of analysis should be a dependency map rather than a single virtual machine. Measure end-to-end response times, traffic volume and direction, peak throughput, and the amount of state that must be synchronized across locations. Google's [hybrid architecture guidance](https://docs.cloud.google.com/architecture/hybrid-multicloud-patterns-and-practices/distributed-patterns) identifies geographic distance and network connectivity as sources of latency, and notes that running components across environments adds management and reliability work. A partial move can therefore erase the advantage of cheaper local compute if the application remains tightly coupled to cloud databases, queues, or identity services.

Portability has a similar boundary. Containers can package an application, but they do not recreate a managed database's backup behavior, a provider's access controls, or a deployment pipeline. Kubernetes may help teams run the same application model in several places; operating its control plane, networking, storage, upgrades, and recovery becomes part of the local bill. The harder question is which provider-specific services must be replaced or retained, and what functionality would be lost in the process.

## Control does not automatically mean compliance

An organization may need a particular data location, audit trail, hardware configuration, or operational boundary. Those requirements can justify private infrastructure, but they must be specified precisely. A regulated workload is not automatically barred from public cloud, and possession of the servers does not itself prove compliance. The relevant test is whether an architecture can satisfy the applicable controls and produce evidence for them.

The responsibility shift is concrete. Under [AWS's shared responsibility model](https://aws.amazon.com/compliance/shared-responsibility-model/), AWS operates the underlying facilities and infrastructure while customers remain responsible for duties that vary by service, including data and application controls. After repatriation, the operator must also arrange physical security, hardware maintenance, platform patching, backup integrity, and recovery capacity, whether directly or through a colocation or private cloud provider. A decision motivated by control should identify who will perform and audit each of those tasks.

## Prove the move with one bounded workload

A candidate is strongest when demand is stable, cloud spending remains high after optimization, dependencies can move together, and the organization can operate the replacement at the required reliability. A traffic pattern with large unpredictable peaks, a small team relying heavily on managed services, or a system that must exchange data continuously with cloud components may favor staying put. These are conditions to test, not categories that settle the decision in advance.

For a proposed move, establish a baseline for cost per useful unit of work, peak and normal load, tail latency, availability, and recovery objectives. Model the same measures for the target environment, including capacity headroom and failure scenarios. Pilot a bounded service or data set, test restore and failover, and measure the real cross-environment traffic before committing to a cutover. A break-even estimate is credible only if its assumptions about utilization, staff time, hardware life, and growth are explicit.

Repatriation is most persuasive when an operator can turn predictable demand and existing operational capability into a durable advantage. The 37signals case shows how existing data center capacity can change the economics; other organizations must establish their own baseline. A cheaper server is useful evidence only when the workload still meets its performance, security, and recovery obligations after the move.
