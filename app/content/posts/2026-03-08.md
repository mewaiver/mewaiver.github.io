The dashboard shows that order creation has slowed. The error rate remains steady, authentication responds normally, and the database does not appear saturated. Metrics, logs, and distributed traces are available. Yet the team cannot tell whether the delay began in the order service, at a connection to an external partner, or in a retry that extended the response time. This situation is hypothetical, but it exposes a real problem: recording the behavior of each component is not the same as explaining the behavior of the system as a whole.

## The symptom crosses boundaries

Monitoring is essential for noticing that something has changed. Latency by route, error rates, and resource saturation help detect impact and decide when to act. Observability requires a further step: asking questions about internal states that no dashboard anticipated and finding evidence to answer them. This distinction does not make dashboards obsolete. As the [Google SRE book](https://sre.google/sre-book/monitoring-distributed-systems/) explains, alerts should prioritize symptoms that matter to users, while internal signals help investigate their causes.

In a distributed call, the time seen by a client may include authentication, queues, queries, network communication, waiting for connections, and external calls. Retries may preserve the success rate while making latency worse. A global average can conceal a problem limited to one route or region. The first operational question, then, is not “which service is broken?” but “which requests slowed down, since when, and under what conditions?” Comparing latency distributions by route and region, traffic volume, errors, and deployments narrows the search before examining an individual request.

The three common signals operate at different scales. **Metrics** aggregate many operations and reveal the scope and progression of a symptom; they do not, on their own, recount a particular request's path. **Logs** record local events and decisions, such as a retry or an exception; without identifiers and context, events from different services can be difficult to connect. **Distributed traces** link instrumented operations within an execution. They show where time was observed, but cannot automatically reveal what happened in an uninstrumented interval or establish the underlying cause of a wait. Their value comes from combining them: metrics select the affected slice, a trace locates a suspect step, and logs help test what happened there.

## What a trace actually shows

A trace is composed of *spans*, records of operations with start and end times, attributes, and identifiers. Spans share a trace identifier; each has its own identifier and can record the span that initiated it. This parent-child relationship helps reconstruct a path across services and attribute observed latency intervals to instrumented operations. If two calls run in parallel, however, adding every span's duration exaggerates the total time. Engineers must examine overlap and the path that limits the response. The [OpenTelemetry trace specification](https://opentelemetry.io/docs/specs/otel/trace/data-model/) describes these relationships as well as *links* for causal connections that do not fit a single parent-child relationship.

Continuity depends on context propagation. When making an HTTP call, the originating service sends identifiers, usually in the W3C `traceparent` header; the next service extracts that context before creating its span. Messaging and asynchronous tasks likewise need to carry or relate the context. If a client, proxy, or consumer stops propagating it, the next segment may start another trace. The interface then displays two partial histories that appear unrelated. A *link* can preserve the connection in batch processing or at a boundary where starting a new trace is deliberate. Propagating identifiers also requires care at trust boundaries, since third parties may have supplied incoming headers. The [OpenTelemetry context propagation documentation](https://opentelemetry.io/docs/concepts/context-propagation/) explains the mechanism; the presence of a header does not, however, guarantee that every library and execution path uses it correctly.

## An investigation that does not end at the chart

Consider, **solely as a hypothetical example**, a `POST /orders` operation that passes through an API gateway, an authentication service, and an order service. The order service queries a database and calls an external partner to reserve inventory. The following times are illustrative, not production measurements: one request takes 2.4 seconds; the authentication span lasts 40 milliseconds, the database query 60 milliseconds, and the partner call 1.8 seconds. The order service eventually responds successfully after a retry. None of these figures identifies the failure on its own.

The team would first check whether the latency increase is concentrated on that route, on a deployed version, or on a particular set of instances. It would also compare traffic and retry rates with the preceding period. If only orders requiring inventory reservation are slower, the next step is to find traces for those requests and check how much of the gateway's measured time appears in instrumented downstream operations. The long interval in the external call makes that boundary a priority for investigation; it does not prove the partner processed the request slowly. Depending on the instrumentation, the client timer may include waiting for an available connection, establishing a connection, transmission, a timeout, and retries.

Structured logs from the order service, with `trace_id` and `span_id` when available, may show how many attempts occurred, their response codes, and whether a timeout occurred. They do not replace metrics for connection-pool occupancy, concurrent calls, or latency observed by the partner. To distinguish local congestion from a slow remote response, engineers would need to separate connection acquisition time from the duration of each attempt, compare those measurements with the partner's records or an independent measurement, and check whether the pattern recurs in other requests. If the partner provides no telemetry, the conclusion must remain limited: a wait was observed at the external boundary, but its internal cause remains unknown. Immediate mitigation, such as changing concurrency or retry policy, must also be assessed for its effect on load and success rates; more attempts may worsen contention.

Google's [Dapper study](https://research.google/pubs/dapper-a-large-scale-distributed-systems-tracing-infrastructure/) illustrates a practical coverage trade-off: instrumenting common libraries makes tracing broadly available at manageable overhead, but custom adapters and queue handoffs still require deliberate measurement. A trace can be complete at its recorded boundaries while omitting the wait that distinguishes two competing explanations.

There is an even earlier check: is the trace complete? A missing span may mean uninstrumented work, broken propagation, export loss, or a sampling decision. The difference between a parent span's duration and the intervals attributed to its children is a clue to investigate, not a ready-made measure of “CPU time” or proof that the service was idle. The same applies to a log without a trace identifier: matching timestamps help find candidates but do not prove that an event belongs to the request. The [OpenTelemetry log data model](https://opentelemetry.io/docs/specs/otel/logs/) includes trace and span identifiers to support that correlation when context is available.

## Standardized collection does not settle the inference

OpenTelemetry provides APIs, SDKs, conventions, and propagation mechanisms for instrumenting applications; its Collector receives, processes, and exports telemetry. By itself, it is neither the storage system nor the interface used to search traces. This separation allows teams to change or combine destinations, but it also creates operational decisions: which libraries to instrument, which attributes to retain, where to run the Collector, how to handle export failures, and how to verify that the data arrives intact. Automatic instrumentation speeds up coverage of common calls; business operations, custom libraries, connection pools, and asynchronous steps may still need manual instrumentation. The [Collector guide](https://opentelemetry.io/docs/collector/) describes its role in receiving and forwarding signals.

More data can make an investigation harder if it cannot be filtered, correlated, and trusted. In metrics, each distinct combination of attributes can produce a time series: using `user_id`, an order number, or a raw URL as a dimension raises cardinality and increases memory and storage costs. Prefer bounded dimensions, such as a parameterized route and an outcome class, and keep order identifiers in logs or traces only when needed and permitted. [Prometheus documentation](https://prometheus.io/docs/practices/naming/) warns about the growth in series from combinations of labels; [OpenTelemetry](https://opentelemetry.io/docs/concepts/signals/metrics/) describes how cardinality affects metric aggregation.

Complete traces also cost processing, transport, and retention. Sampling at the start of a request (*head sampling*) reduces volume early but may discard a rare event that becomes interesting only later. Sampling after spans arrive (*tail sampling*) can prioritize slow or erroneous traces, at the cost of temporarily holding spans, bringing parts of the same execution together, and operating a stateful component. Even that strategy has limited value if the decisive step was never instrumented or arrived too late. The [OpenTelemetry sampling documentation](https://opentelemetry.io/docs/concepts/sampling/) explains these costs and limitations. A policy must fit the kind of failure under investigation, the budget, and the capacity of the telemetry pipeline itself; a sample adequate for general trends may be inadequate for a rare incident.

The cost is not only financial. Instrumentation consumes application resources; batch export can reduce work on the request path, but full queues or Collector failures can cause data loss and need monitoring. Keeping detailed traces for less time reduces cost and exposure, but shortens the window for investigating older incidents. Request bodies, tokens, and personal identifiers should not be added to attributes or logs for convenience. Filtering later may help, but preventing capture is safer: a copy already emitted may have passed through queues, storage, and vendors. [OpenTelemetry's guidance on handling sensitive data](https://opentelemetry.io/docs/security/handling-sensitive-data/) places responsibility for identifying and protecting that data on the team. Excessive alerts create another operational cost: when every fluctuation pages someone, less attention remains for real incidents. Reserve alerts for actionable symptoms and use detailed telemetry during the investigation instead of turning every internal metric into a notification.

## The engineering decision starts with the question

An observability strategy begins with the decisions a team must make during a failure: recognize impact, identify the affected requests, locate the slow step, and distinguish competing explanations. Those needs determine where to measure distributions, which boundaries require spans, which events merit structured logs, and how long each signal should be retained. They also show when broader coverage or more selective sampling is worth the cost. The approach should be tested against known failures and revised as the architecture changes; a dashboard that accurately described the old request path can mislead after the addition of a queue or an external partner.

Distributed systems remain difficult to diagnose because telemetry is a partial observation produced by instruments with their own boundaries, losses, and costs. Metrics detect patterns, traces organize the observed execution, and logs record local decisions. Diagnosis depends on knowing what each piece of evidence can establish, deliberately looking for what is missing, and measuring the boundary that can separate one explanation from another. It is this discipline of inference, rather than the volume of collected data, that turns operational data into understanding.
