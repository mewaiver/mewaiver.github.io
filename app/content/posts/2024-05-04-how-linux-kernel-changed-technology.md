The checkout dashboard turns red. The API gateway is returning successful responses, but customers are waiting longer to place orders. Authentication, order processing, a database, and a payment provider all sit on the request path. The team's own services emit metrics, logs, and traces; the provider may expose little of its internal behavior. The on-call engineer can see the delay without yet knowing which component introduced it, or whether the slow component is itself waiting on something else.

This is a hypothetical incident, and every number below is illustrative. It exposes the central difficulty of distributed diagnosis: telemetry records selected observations from several processes, while an explanation must connect those observations to a causal mechanism. A long payment span, for example, can include time spent acquiring a local connection, crossing the network, waiting at the provider, or retrying after a timeout. Calling the provider "the root cause" from that span alone would confuse a boundary measurement with an explanation.

## A symptom is easier to measure than a cause

Monitoring is well suited to detecting a user-visible change. A latency histogram grouped by route can show that the checkout endpoint's 95th percentile has risen, while request and error counts establish its scope. An alert on that symptom can bring an engineer to the system before a known internal failure mode has been identified. Observability, in the operational sense used here, is the ability to investigate the behavior behind the change, including failure modes that nobody anticipated when building the dashboard. The boundary is practical rather than absolute: monitoring systems can also support debugging. Google's [SRE discussion of symptoms and causes](https://sre.google/sre-book/monitoring-distributed-systems/) makes clear why an alert and a root-cause investigation ask different questions.

In a distributed request, each service sees only part of the work. A gateway may report 1.6 seconds for a checkout while the order service reports a much shorter database query. Neither measurement is wrong. The gateway's timing can include downstream calls and, depending on where measurement starts, time spent in its own queue; the database metric covers only the work measured at that boundary. Retries, parallel calls, connection pools, asynchronous handoffs, and unsynchronized clocks further complicate a request timeline. A dashboard that aggregates each service independently can show several correlated slowdowns without showing which delay came first or which one is merely a consequence.

## What the three signals can establish

Metrics summarize populations. Request latency distributions, traffic, errors, and saturation can identify when an incident started and whether it affects one route, region, or service version. Aggregation is also their limitation: a percentile does not reveal the sequence of operations in any particular request. Averages can conceal a small group of very slow checkouts, and a single slow trace cannot establish how common that path is.

Logs capture discrete decisions and failures: a retry, a pool-acquisition timeout, a rejected payment response. Structured fields such as service name, route, attempt number, and trace ID make them searchable across processes. They can explain *what a component decided* if the relevant event was logged, but a pile of timestamped messages does not reconstruct unlogged work or establish ordering across hosts with imperfect clocks. Logging every payload would add volume and could expose customer data without making the missing causal step visible. OpenTelemetry's [log guidance](https://opentelemetry.io/docs/concepts/signals/logs/) describes trace and span identifiers as a way to correlate logs with the request being investigated.

A trace groups spans that represent operations such as gateway handling, authentication, a database query, and an outbound HTTP call. A span records its start and end, attributes, and a parent relationship. Shared trace IDs and parent span IDs let an engineer follow work across services and attribute elapsed time to observed boundaries. They do not imply that all child durations can be added together: siblings may run in parallel, and an instrumented operation may contain uninstrumented waiting. The [OpenTelemetry trace model](https://opentelemetry.io/docs/specs/otel/trace/data-model/) also distinguishes an absent span from a broken causal link: a service may propagate context while dropping its span, leaving the collected trace incomplete.

Those relationships depend on context propagation. An instrumented HTTP client injects trace context into an outgoing request; the receiving service extracts it and creates a child span. The W3C [`traceparent` specification](https://www.w3.org/TR/trace-context/) defines a common HTTP format, while [OpenTelemetry's propagation documentation](https://opentelemetry.io/docs/concepts/context-propagation/) describes the extraction and injection process. If a custom client, queue consumer, or background task fails to carry context, the next service may start a new trace. Both services can be instrumented and still appear unrelated. A trace's apparent end may then mark an instrumentation gap rather than the end of the work.

## Following a slow checkout without inventing a culprit

Suppose, in the hypothetical system, checkout's 95th-percentile latency rises from 250 milliseconds to 1.6 seconds after a deployment, while completed checkouts and error rate remain roughly steady. The first useful question is whether the increase is limited to checkout and the new version. Gateway metrics answer the scope question; order-service latency and database query distributions narrow the boundary. If authentication and database timings remain near their earlier ranges, they become less likely explanations for the newly added delay, though a service-level aggregate could still hide a small affected subset.

Now take several *sampled* slow checkouts from the affected route and version. Imagine that their order spans last about 1.5 seconds and contain an outbound payment-client span lasting about 1.3 seconds. The trace locates most observed elapsed time at that call boundary. It does not distinguish provider processing from local connection-pool wait, DNS or TLS setup, network loss, or retries hidden inside the client library. Correlated order-service logs might show a timeout followed by a second payment attempt for the same trace ID. That makes retry behavior a plausible contributor; it does not yet explain why the first attempt timed out.

The next measurements should separate those possibilities. Record pool occupancy and acquisition time, distinguish each outbound attempt in spans or logs, and compare the client's send-to-receive timing with provider-side request timing if the provider supplies it. Compare affected and unaffected service versions under similar traffic, including configuration changes to timeout and pool limits. A rise in local pool wait with stable provider processing would favor a client-side bottleneck; matching rises in provider processing would favor a downstream one. If neither side has the needed timing, the honest incident report should say where the evidence stops. A controlled mitigation or rollback can strengthen a hypothesis, but temporal coincidence alone is not proof of a single root cause.

## Standard instrumentation is only the beginning

OpenTelemetry supplies APIs, SDKs, instrumentation libraries, context propagation, and conventions for emitting telemetry. Its [Collector](https://opentelemetry.io/docs/collector/) can receive, process, and export signals; it is a pipeline component, not the storage, query engine, or visualization backend that investigators use. This separation helps teams change where data is sent, but it also creates configuration to operate. Auto-instrumentation may cover framework HTTP calls while missing a custom queue envelope, business decision, or third-party client. Engineers adopting it should test one representative request end to end, verify service identity and span relationships, and confirm that logs and metrics use compatible route and version dimensions. Otherwise, a well-populated backend can still present disconnected evidence.

The telemetry pipeline itself can fail under load. Export queues can fill, spans can be dropped, and delayed data can arrive after an investigator's first query. [OpenTelemetry's Collector scaling guidance](https://opentelemetry.io/docs/collector/scaling/) recommends watching queue and refusal metrics and explains why stateful processing needs particular care. This matters most during incidents, when traffic patterns and error logs may change sharply. An apparently clean trace is less reassuring if the collector was dropping data in the same interval.

## The cost of seeing, and of missing, detail

More dimensions make metrics useful for comparison, but every unique label combination creates another time series. [Prometheus's metric guidance](https://prometheus.io/docs/practices/naming/) warns against unbounded labels such as user IDs and email addresses. Route templates and bounded service-version labels can support incident slicing; raw URLs or customer IDs can multiply storage and query cost while creating a privacy problem. Per-request identifiers belong in carefully controlled traces or logs when needed for correlation, subject to retention and access rules. Short retention may save money but erase the baseline needed to compare a rare incident with an earlier one; long retention increases both cost and exposure.

Sampling makes the same trade-off for traces. Head sampling decides early and is relatively cheap, but it cannot guarantee capture of a rare slow or erroneous request discovered only at the end. Tail sampling can retain traces based on observed latency or errors, but it must buffer spans and route all spans of a trace to the same decision point; split routing can produce partial traces. These are documented in OpenTelemetry's [sampling](https://opentelemetry.io/docs/concepts/sampling/) and [Collector scaling](https://opentelemetry.io/docs/collector/scaling/) guidance. Teams should choose rates and retention from the incidents they need to investigate, then check whether the chosen policy actually preserves representative slow paths. Sampling also changes the evidence base: an unusual trace is useful for mechanism, while independently collected request metrics are needed to judge prevalence.

Instrumentation, export, storage, and querying consume CPU, memory, network capacity, and operator attention. Sensitive values can leak through log bodies, span attributes, and propagated headers; the W3C specification explicitly discusses [trace-context privacy and security](https://www.w3.org/TR/trace-context/#privacy-considerations). Alert rules add another cost when they page for every internal anomaly rather than a meaningful user symptom. A sensible design therefore starts with user-facing service indicators, keeps enough bounded dimensions to find affected cohorts, and spends detailed telemetry on the boundaries where competing explanations would otherwise be indistinguishable. The right balance depends on traffic, incident frequency, privacy requirements, and the team's ability to operate the pipeline.

Distributed systems remain difficult to diagnose because their evidence is partial and their delays cross ownership and process boundaries. Metrics establish scope, traces locate measured work, and logs reveal selected decisions; none converts correlation into causation on its own. The most valuable observability investment is the one that lets an engineer test a specific alternative explanation at the next uncertain boundary. When the evidence cannot make that distinction, the gap itself is an engineering finding.
